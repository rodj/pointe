Pointe Shoe Foot Care: Blisters, Bunions & Toenails

Updated August 2026

Dancing on pointe puts your entire body weight onto a few square centimetres of toe. Friction, pressure, and repetition are unavoidable, so some wear and tear on your feet is part of the work. What is not inevitable is the damage that comes from ignoring small problems until they force you out of class.

This guide covers the everyday foot care that keeps dancers in their shoes: how to prevent and treat blisters, what to do about bunions and corns, how to look after toenails, and the signs that mean you should stop and see a professional.

Read this first: this is general guidance for healthy dancers, not medical advice. Anything that involves persistent pain, an infection, or a change in how you walk belongs with a doctor or podiatrist — ideally one who works with dancers.

The Daily Routine That Prevents Most Problems

Most dancer foot problems are friction problems, and friction is manageable. A short daily routine prevents far more than any treatment fixes.

  • Wash and dry your feet properly. Especially between the toes. Damp skin blisters faster and is more hospitable to fungal infections, which thrive inside a warm shoe.
  • Keep toenails short and square. Trim straight across, level with the tip of the toe — not curved down at the corners. Long nails jam into the box on pointe; nails cut into the corners invite ingrowth.
  • Manage calluses, don't remove them. A moderate callus is armour that your foot built for you. File it flat when it gets thick or ragged, but never shave it off — bare skin underneath blisters immediately.
  • Moisturise at night, not before class. Soft skin at the barre tears; dry cracked skin tears too. Moisturise in the evening so the skin is supple but not slick by morning.
  • Air your shoes out after every class. Damp shoes soften faster and hold bacteria. This is good for your feet and for the shoes — see our guide on making pointe shoes last longer.
  • Check your feet weekly. Look at the pressure points, the nail beds, and the skin between the toes. Problems caught early are small problems.

Blisters: Prevention First

Blisters form where skin slides against fabric repeatedly. The fix is to reduce the sliding, not to toughen up and push through it.

Reducing friction

Fit is the biggest lever. A shoe that is slightly too big lets your foot move inside it, and that movement is exactly what causes blisters. If you are blistering in the same spot every single class, that is a fit signal — take it to your fitter rather than treating the symptom forever. Our fitting guide covers what to look for.

Tape the hot spots before they blister. Most dancers know their two or three problem areas. Cover them with a smooth, low-friction tape before class rather than waiting for the skin to break. Smooth every edge down — a wrinkle in the tape becomes a new pressure point.

Get your padding right. Toe pads spread pressure across the whole forefoot instead of concentrating it on one or two toes. More padding is not automatically better: too much bulk makes the shoe feel vague and can create new rub points. Use the thinnest padding you can dance well in.

Keep your feet dry. Damp skin blisters far more readily. If you sweat heavily, a light dusting of powder before class or a spare pair of toe pads for the second half makes a real difference.

Treating a blister that has already formed

An intact blister is a sterile dressing your body made. Leave it alone if you possibly can — pad around it with a doughnut-shaped piece of moleskin so the shoe presses on the ring, not the blister.

If it is large, painful, and in a place that will burst anyway, drain it cleanly: wash your hands and the area, sterilise a needle, pierce the edge, press the fluid out, and leave the roof of the skin in place. That skin protects the raw layer underneath. Cover with a clean dressing.

If the blister has already torn open, clean it, cover it, and change the dressing daily. Watch for spreading redness, heat, swelling, pus, or a red streak running up the foot — those mean infection and a same-day doctor's visit, not another class.

Blood blisters: a dark blister under a nail or on a toe pad means bleeding under the skin, usually from a sharp impact or a nail that is too long. Do not drain it yourself — deeper tissue is involved and the infection risk is higher. Pad around it and get it looked at if it is painful or growing.

Toenails: The Most Common Avoidable Injury

Nail problems account for a huge share of dancers' lost class time, and nearly all of them trace back to nail length or shoe length.

Black toenails are bruises under the nail, caused by the nail repeatedly hitting the end of the box. Sometimes that is a nail cut too long; often it is a shoe that lets the foot travel forward. The bruise itself usually just has to grow out. If it is throbbing under pressure, a doctor can relieve it — this is not a bathroom procedure.

Ingrown toenails come from cutting nails curved at the corners or from sustained side pressure in a narrow box. Early on, soaking in warm water and easing the pressure off the side of the toe is often enough. Once the area is red, swollen, and tender, it is infected and needs a podiatrist. Digging at the corner yourself reliably makes it worse.

Thickened or discoloured nails that persist for months are frequently fungal — an occupational hazard of warm damp shoes. Over-the-counter treatments are slow and unreliable on toenails; a doctor can confirm what it actually is and prescribe something that works.

The prevention is the same in all three cases: trim straight across level with the toe tip, file the edges smooth so they do not catch on tights, and make sure the shoe is not letting your foot slide forward onto the nails.

Bunions, Corns, and Calluses

Bunions

A bunion is a structural change at the joint of the big toe, where the joint drifts outward and the toe angles in toward the others. Genetics and foot shape are the main drivers — ballet does not create bunions out of nothing, but pressure from a narrow box on an already-susceptible joint can make one more painful and more prominent.

You cannot reverse a bunion without surgery, and dancers rarely want that surgery mid-career. What you can do is stop provoking it:

  • Make sure your box is wide enough at the joint. This is the single most useful change, and it usually means a conversation with your fitter about width and box shape rather than a different brand.
  • Use a gel bunion shield to cushion the joint against the box wall.
  • Use a toe spacer between the big and second toes to reduce the inward pull on the toe.
  • Strengthen the intrinsic foot muscles and work on alignment — rolling in through the arch loads the joint harder with every step.

If the joint is painful at rest, stiffening, or changing shape noticeably, see a podiatrist before it limits what you can dance.

Corns

Corns are small cones of hardened skin over a concentrated pressure point, most often on the top or side of a toe where it meets the box. They hurt because the hard core presses inward.

Treat the cause: pad the spot, check whether the toe is being crowded by a box that is too shallow or too narrow, and use a spacer if a neighbouring toe is doing the pressing. Soften the corn with regular moisturising and gentle filing after a bath. Do not cut a corn out — that is how a minor nuisance becomes an infection.

Calluses

Calluses are your friends up to a point. You want a smooth, even layer of thickened skin over the working surfaces of the toes. You do not want a thick ridged callus, because the hard edge shears against the softer skin beneath it and blisters under the callus — which is far more painful than an ordinary blister and much slower to heal.

File calluses flat and smooth after a shower, a little at a time, and stop while there is still a protective layer. Never take them down to bare skin the week of a performance.

Foot Care Essentials

You do not need a large kit. These are the items that earn their place in a dance bag.

Toe Tape

A smooth, flexible tape for covering hot spots before they blister. This is the highest-value item on the list — cheap, light, and it prevents the problem you would otherwise spend weeks treating. Keep a roll in your bag permanently and tape before class, not after the skin breaks.

Gel Toe Pads

Cushioning that spreads load across the whole forefoot instead of a couple of toes. Gel is more durable and more protective than lambswool but slightly bulkier, so buy them when you can try the shoe with them in. Replace them when the gel thins out at the pressure points.

Toe Spacers

Small gel or foam wedges that sit between the toes to stop them pressing on each other. Useful for bunion pain, for corns caused by a neighbouring toe, and for anyone whose toes crowd together in the box. Inexpensive and worth trying before more drastic measures.

Gel Bunion Shields

A cushioned sleeve or pad over the big-toe joint that takes the friction from the box wall. It will not correct a bunion, but it can make the difference between a comfortable class and a painful one while you sort out the fit.

Foot File or Pumice Stone

For keeping calluses flat and smooth rather than thick and ridged. Use it after a shower when the skin is soft, and take off less than you think you need to.

Rolling the sole of the foot after class relieves tension through the arch and plantar fascia, which take a beating on pointe. Two minutes per foot at the end of the day is enough to notice the difference.

Recovery After Class

What you do in the hour after class matters as much as what you do in it.

  1. Get the shoes off promptly. Change into flat shoes and let both feet and pointe shoes dry out.
  2. Check for damage. Look at the toes and nail beds while the memory of what hurt is fresh. Treat any broken skin before it sits overnight in a sock.
  3. Roll out the sole of each foot. A ball or roller for a couple of minutes per foot releases the arch.
  4. Stretch the calves and the toes. Tight calves change how you load the forefoot and quietly contribute to foot pain.
  5. Elevate if the feet are swollen. Ten minutes with your feet up after a heavy rehearsal day is genuinely useful.
  6. Moisturise before bed. Supple skin resists tearing; slick skin at the barre does not.

When to Stop and See a Professional

Dancers are trained to work through discomfort, which makes it hard to notice the point where discomfort has become injury. Stop and get assessed if you have any of these:

  • Pain that persists when you are not dancing, or that wakes you at night
  • Sharp or localised pain in a bone rather than diffuse muscle soreness — particularly along the top of the foot, which can indicate a stress fracture
  • Any redness that spreads, warmth, swelling, pus, or fever after a broken blister or nail injury
  • Numbness, tingling, or pins and needles in the toes that continues after the shoes come off
  • A joint that is visibly changing shape or losing range of motion
  • An ankle that gives way or rolls, rather than simply feeling tired
  • The same injury recurring in the same place despite padding and taping — that is a fit or technique problem, and no amount of tape will fix it

A podiatrist or physiotherapist who works with dancers is worth finding before you need one. They will assess your technique and your shoes as part of the picture, rather than simply telling you to stop dancing.

The Bottom Line

Good foot care is unglamorous and mostly preventive: short square nails, dry feet, tape on the hot spots before they blister, padding that spreads pressure without adding bulk, and a shoe that actually fits. Nearly every recurring foot problem in pointe shoes is telling you something about the fit — treat the symptom for today's class, then go and fix the cause.
