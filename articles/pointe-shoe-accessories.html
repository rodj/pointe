Pointe Shoe Accessories Every Dancer Needs (2026 Guide)

You've been fitted for your first pair of pointe shoes — congratulations. Now comes the part nobody warned you about: you need a small collection of pointe shoe accessories before you can actually dance in them. Ribbons, elastic, toe pads, a sewing kit, and a few other essentials aren't optional. Most pointe shoes ship bare — no ribbons attached, no padding included.

This guide covers every accessory worth buying, explains which types work best for different feet, and tells you exactly what to skip. If you're still choosing shoes, start with our best pointe shoes guide and fitting guide first, then come back here.

Toe Pads: Your Most Important Accessory

Toe pads sit between your toes and the inside of the box. They cushion impact, reduce friction, and prevent blisters. Every dancer on pointe uses some form of toe protection — the debate is over which type.

There are three main categories:

Gel toe pads offer the most cushioning. They're made from a soft silicone gel that absorbs shock well. The trade-off is thickness — gel pads take up space inside the box, which can affect your fit and reduce the floor feel that advanced dancers rely on. For beginners, this cushioning is usually worth it.

Fabric toe pads are thinner and allow more articulation through the shoe. They provide moderate protection and breathe better than gel. Many intermediate and advanced dancers prefer fabric pads because they give a closer connection to the floor without sacrificing all protection.

Silicone toe caps are individual sleeves that fit over each toe. They're useful for targeting specific problem toes — a longer second toe that takes too much pressure, for instance — without adding bulk everywhere else.

Tip: Your toe pads affect your fit. Always bring the toe pads you plan to use when getting fitted for pointe shoes. If you switch pad types later, your shoes may feel tighter or looser. See our fitting guide for more on this.

Toe Spacers and Bunion Guards

Toe spacers are small gel or silicone wedges that sit between toes to maintain alignment inside the shoe. They're especially important if your big toe and second toe crowd together on pointe, which creates friction and can lead to bunions over time.

Bunion guards are soft gel pads that cover the bony bump on the side of the big toe joint. Even if you don't have a bunion yet, the repetitive pressure of pointe work on that joint makes prevention worthwhile. They're thin enough that they won't alter your fit significantly.

Ribbons and Elastic

Most pointe shoes don't come with ribbons or elastic — you sew them on yourself. You need both: ribbons wrap around the ankle for aesthetics and support, while elastic keeps the heel of the shoe snug against your foot.

Satin ribbon is the traditional choice. It looks clean onstage and provides firm support. The downside is that it doesn't give at all, so you need to tie it at exactly the right tension every time.

Stretch ribbon (sometimes called elasticized ribbon) has become increasingly popular. It flexes with your ankle through plié and relevé, which means fewer pressure points and less fiddling with tension. Many teachers now prefer stretch ribbon for students because it's more forgiving.

For elastic, you want 3/4-inch width. It crosses the front of the ankle and keeps the shoe from gapping at the heel. Some dancers use a single piece of elastic; others use two shorter pieces on either side of the heel seam. Your teacher will have a preference — ask before you sew.

Tip: Match your ribbon color to your shoe color and tights. Pink satin ribbon with pink shoes and pink tights creates a clean, unbroken line from leg to toe — that's the goal. Some companies make ribbons in different shades to match various skin tones for a seamless look.

Sewing Kit for Pointe Shoes

You'll sew ribbons and elastic onto every pair of pointe shoes you own. A basic kit needs just three things: a strong needle (curved or straight — curved is easier for beginners), heavy-duty thread that matches your shoe color, and a thimble. Pointe shoe satin is tough to push a needle through, and the layers of fabric at the heel seam are even tougher. Don't skip the thimble.

Pre-made pointe shoe sewing kits include everything sized correctly. They're inexpensive and save you from guessing at the hardware store.

Pointe Shoe Bag

Pointe shoes absorb a lot of sweat. Stuffing damp shoes into a sealed dance bag is a fast track to odor and premature breakdown of the paste and materials. A mesh pointe shoe bag allows airflow so your shoes can start drying immediately after class.

This is a small investment that extends shoe life. Given that pointe shoes cost $60–$100+ per pair, anything that adds even a few extra classes of wear pays for itself quickly.

Rosin

Rosin is a tree-resin powder that you apply to the platform (flat tip) of your pointe shoes to prevent slipping. Most studios have a rosin box near the door, but not all of them. Having your own small container means you're never without it.

You can also apply rosin to the sole of the shoe for extra grip. Use it sparingly — too much makes the floor sticky and can actually increase the risk of a twisted ankle by preventing controlled slides.

Tip: Some studios use rosin alternatives like Jet Glue on the platforms or a spritz of water on the soles. Check with your teacher about what's preferred at your studio before coating your shoes in rosin on day one.

Lamb's Wool

Before gel pads existed, lamb's wool was the standard toe protection for pointe dancers — and many professionals still prefer it. It's natural, moldable, and breathable. You pull off a small amount, wrap it around and between your toes, and it conforms to your exact foot shape.

The advantage over gel pads is customization: you use more wool where you need more cushioning and less where you don't. The disadvantage is that it takes practice to wrap consistently, and it compresses over time so you need to replace it regularly.

Lamb's wool is worth trying even if you primarily use gel pads. Many dancers use a combination — gel pads for overall cushioning with a wisp of lamb's wool tucked around a problem toe.

Theraband / Resistance Bands for Foot Strengthening

Strong feet are the foundation of safe pointe work. Therabands (resistance bands) are the single most effective tool for building the intrinsic foot muscles, ankle stability, and arch strength that pointe demands. Your teacher likely already has you doing theraband exercises — having your own means you can do them at home daily.

Start with medium resistance (typically red or green, depending on the brand). Wrap the band around the ball of your foot and work through slow, controlled points and flexes. Add relevé exercises and ankle circles as you progress. Consistent theraband work noticeably improves how your feet look and feel in pointe shoes within weeks.

Toe Tape / Athletic Tape

Tape is a targeted fix for specific friction points. If one toe keeps blistering, tape it. If your big toe joint is rubbing raw against the box, tape over it. Athletic tape (or paper medical tape for sensitive skin) applied directly to the skin creates a protective barrier that's thinner and more precise than any pad.

Many dancers tape their toes as a supplement to toe pads, not a replacement. The tape goes on first, then the toe pad goes over everything. This layered approach covers both the friction points between toes (where pads don't reach) and the broader contact areas against the shoe.

Pre-wrap (the thin foam used under athletic tape) is another option. It's gentler on skin and doesn't leave adhesive residue, but it shifts more easily during class.

The Starter Kit: What New Pointe Students Actually Need

If you're buying accessories for your first pair of pointe shoes, you don't need everything on this list right away. Here's the minimum:

  1. Toe pads — Gel pads (like the Ouch Pouch) are the safest bet for beginners. Bring them to your shoe fitting.
  2. Ribbons and elastic — Stretch ribbon is more forgiving for new students. Get elastic too.
  3. Sewing kit — You need to sew ribbons and elastic on before your first class.
  4. Toe tape — A roll of athletic tape for blisters and hot spots. You'll discover where you need it after your first few classes.
  5. Theraband — Start strengthening exercises immediately. Your teacher will thank you.
Tip: Budget approximately $40–$60 for starter accessories on top of the cost of the shoes themselves. It's tempting to buy everything at once, but start with the basics above and add items like lamb's wool, spacers, or a mesh bag as you learn what your feet need. Every dancer's feet are different — what your classmate swears by might not work for you.

Total estimated starter cost: $45–$55 for the five essentials listed above, plus $60–$100+ for the shoes themselves.

Final Notes

Pointe shoe accessories aren't glamorous, but they directly affect how long you can dance comfortably and how long your shoes last. A $12 mesh bag that adds five extra classes to a pair of shoes saves you real money. A $5 roll of tape that prevents a blister keeps you in class instead of sitting out.

Buy quality basics, experiment to find what works for your feet, and replace worn-out items before they fail. Your feet — and your dancing — will reflect the care you put in.
